## The formula

> mph = km/h × 0.621371

The number comes from the definition of the mile. One international mile is
exactly 1,609.344 metres, fixed by international agreement in 1959. So one
kilometre is 1 ÷ 1.609344 = 0.621371 miles, and the ratio carries directly into
speeds because both units share the same hour.

## Doing it in your head

The reliable trick is **multiply by 6 and drop a digit**, which approximates
0.6 rather than 0.621 — so it under-reads by about 3 percent.

- 100 km/h → 100 × 6 = 600 → 60 mph (true answer 62.1)
- 50 km/h → 300 → 30 mph (true 31.1)

For better accuracy, add back a twentieth: 60 + 3 = 63, against a true 62.1.

A neater trick uses the Fibonacci sequence, because consecutive Fibonacci numbers
approach the golden ratio 1.618, which is remarkably close to 1.609:

- 5 km/h ≈ 3 mph, 8 km/h ≈ 5 mph, 13 km/h ≈ 8 mph, 21 km/h ≈ 13 mph

These are accurate to under 1 percent.

## Which countries use which

Only three countries use miles per hour on road signs as standard: the **United
Kingdom**, the **United States** and **Liberia**. Everywhere else uses km/h.

The UK is a hybrid worth watching out for — road signs and car speedometers are
in mph, but fuel is sold in litres and distances in official documents are often
metric.

## Where the kilometre came from

The metre was defined in 1793 by the French Academy of Sciences as one
ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, measured along
the meridian through Paris. The survey took seven years and, as it turned out,
contained a small error — the Earth is slightly flatter than the surveyors
assumed — so the original metre was about 0.2 mm short.

Rather than redo it, the length was kept and progressively redefined: first
against a platinum bar, then against a wavelength of krypton-86 light, and since
1983 in terms of the speed of light, which is now *defined* as exactly
299,792,458 metres per second. The metre is whatever distance light covers in
1/299,792,458 of a second.

The mile has an older and messier lineage. It descends from the Roman *mille
passus*, a thousand paces of two steps each, roughly 1,480 m. English statute law
in 1593 fixed the mile at eight furlongs — a furlong being the length of a
ploughed furrow — giving 5,280 feet, which is why the number is so unmemorable.

## Conversions in sport

Athletics runs almost entirely in metric, but a few imperial distances survive
and the comparisons are a common source of confusion:

| Event | Distance | Imperial equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| 1,500 m | 1.5 km | 0.93 miles |
| Mile | 1.609 km | 1 mile |
| 5,000 m | 5 km | 3.11 miles |
| 10,000 m | 10 km | 6.21 miles |
| Half marathon | 21.098 km | 13.11 miles |
| Marathon | 42.195 km | 26.22 miles |

The gap between 1,500 m and the mile is 109 m, which is why the two records are
kept separately and why the "four-minute mile" has no metric equivalent.

## Frequently asked

### Is 100 km/h the same as 60 mph?

Close but not equal. 100 km/h is 62.1 mph. The rough equivalence is useful for
estimating, but at motorway speeds a 2 mph error is enough to matter near a
limit.

### What is 1 km/h in mph?

0.621 mph. Conversely 1 mph is 1.609 km/h.

### Why do some speedometers show both?

Cars sold in markets that border a country using the other system usually carry
both scales. Canadian and Mexican cars often show mph inside the km/h ring for
drivers crossing into the United States, and UK-market cars show km/h for drivers
taking the ferry to France.
