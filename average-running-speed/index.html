Average running speed

A recreational runner averages 8–10 km/h, or roughly 6:00–7:30 per km. A well-trained club runner sits nearer 12–14 km/h. Elite marathoners hold about 20 km/h for two hours, which is the part most people fail to appreciate.

Runners think in pace — minutes per kilometre — rather than speed, so both are given throughout.

Pace fades with distance

The single most useful thing to understand about running speed is that it is not one number. The same runner's sustainable pace drops predictably as distance rises:

DistanceTypical fade from 5K pace
1 mileabout 5% faster
5Kbaseline
10K3 – 5% slower
Half marathon8 – 10% slower
Marathon15 – 20% slower

So a runner who races 5K at 5:00/km would expect roughly 5:15/km for 10K, 5:25/km for a half, and 5:45–6:00/km for a marathon. Runners who ignore this and start a marathon at half-marathon pace produce the sport's most reliable disaster.

What changes your pace

Heat and humidity. Larger than most people allow for. Above about 20 °C performance degrades measurably, and humidity compounds it by blocking evaporative cooling. A hot marathon can cost 10–20 minutes against the same fitness in cool conditions.

Elevation. Both altitude (thinner air, less oxygen) and gradient. A rule of thumb many runners use is 10–20 seconds per km slower for every 1% of average gradient.

Surface. Trail running is substantially slower than road for the same effort — uneven footing, obstacles and gradient combine. Comparing trail pace to road pace is close to meaningless.

Fatigue and training state. Obvious, but worth stating: pace on tired legs mid-training-block is not a measure of fitness.

Why GPS pace jumps around

If your watch or phone swings from 4:30 to 6:10 on steady effort, that is the GPS, not you. Position error of a few metres translates into a large apparent pace change when sampled every second.

Self Speed averages over 2.5 seconds, which removes most of the jitter while still responding quickly to genuine changes. Total distance stays accurate because the random errors cancel over time.

Tracks are the exception. A 400 m track is the worst possible GPS environment — tight continuous bends where the systematic error is comparable to the lane width. For track sessions, use a stopwatch. See running speed tracker.
